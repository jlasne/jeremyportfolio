Wealth Architecture

Notes on holding money through a change of era, written for someone starting from zero. Three teachers, Ray Dalio, Charles Gave and Richard Détente, folded into one line of thought.

A Sunday evening in 1971

Ray Dalio was a young clerk on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange when President Nixon went on television and ended the exchange of dollars for gold. Until that evening a dollar was a cheque you could cash in metal. The United States had written more cheques than it had gold, so it stopped honouring them. Dalio came in early on Monday, ready for a crash. The market opened up, and gained close to 25% over the following months.

He went looking for a precedent and found the same evening in 1933, with Roosevelt on the radio instead of Nixon. Same broken promise, same rise in stocks. Then 2008, then 2020. Once you have seen the pattern it is plain: when a state runs short, it prints. New money, with the same wealth behind it, makes each dollar worth less, and the price of things that already exist, shares, gold, oil, copper, goes up.

That is the first thing a saver needs to know, and it changes the question. The beginner asks how to make money grow. The older question is what money is losing against. Richard Détente counts about 8% of new dollars printed each year. Anything held in dollars needs more than 8% a year to stand still, and every product sold as prudent pays less than that.

Dalio's count is wider. Of about 750 currencies that have existed since 1700, fewer than 20% still exist, and each survivor buys less than it did. Détente gives a currency a working life of around 70 years. The dollar system we live in was signed at Bretton Woods in 1944.

Why it keeps happening

Dalio spent 50 years investing, then studied 500 years of history to understand the surprises that had hurt him. The Dutch, the British and the Americans each rose and fell along the same arc, over about 250 years. Between one order and the next came 10 to 20 years of trouble.

The arc has a logic, and it reads like a family story. A war ends and the winner writes the rules. Peace follows, because the winner is too strong to fight. Peace brings trade, trade brings a currency everyone accepts, and a currency everyone accepts lets the country borrow more than anyone else. People grow used to good times and borrow against them. The rich pull away from the rest. Then the debt turns out to be larger than the income, the state prints, prices rise, rich and poor fight over who pays, and a younger power notices the weakness.

Dalio measures the arc with eight powers, numbered in the figure. They rise in order: education first, then invention, competitiveness, output, share of world trade, military strength, the financial centre, and last the reserve currency. He watches for three things arriving together. Debt too large to repay, so the central bank prints. A wealth gap turning into populism on both sides. A rising power facing the leading one. The last time all three lined up was 1930 to 1945. All three are present now.

Détente reads the same cycle in a single number. Take the United States policy interest rate from 1950. It climbs for 30 years, peaks near 1980, then falls for 40 years to zero. Lay the old saying over it: hard times make strong men, strong men make good times, good times make weak men, weak men make hard times. The rate is the temperature of the cycle, and it has been at the bottom of the thermometer.

So the first answer to "will tomorrow look like yesterday" comes from the century. The order that shaped the last 80 years is late in its arc. The turn carries no date, and that is exactly why the rest of these notes exist.

Which season it is

The century tells you the era. It says little about this year. For that, Charles Gave uses two questions he has been refining since 1978. Is the economy growing? Are prices rising? Two answers give four squares, and he found that each square has one asset that does well in it.

Growth without inflation is the good square: stocks, and above all the efficient companies, often technology. Growth with inflation: gold, with stocks and metals alongside. Recession with inflation is the square Gave calls the most unpleasant: cash, in a currency run by serious people, which for him means the Swiss franc. Recession without inflation: government bonds, because the state's promise is the one thing gaining value.

The hard part is knowing which square you are in, since official numbers get revised and massaged. Gave's answer took him 40 years, and it comes from markets, where millions of people vote with money every day.

For growth, divide the S&P 500 by the price of oil and average it over 7 years. His reasoning is physical: an economy is energy turned into things. If companies turn oil into profit, the ratio rises. For inflation, divide the price of gold by the price of long government bonds, over the same 7 years. Both are stores of value. One is offered by the state; the other is what people buy when they stop believing the state.

Seven years matters. In Gave's 100-year test, shorter averages threw false signals, and seven caught every large turn while ignoring the noise. Here Dalio and Gave meet. Dalio's printing is the reason the gold dial swings. Gave's ratio is how you watch it happen from your kitchen table, with the market as your only source.

Gave then does something that looks backwards. He picks a portfolio by what he leaves out. Of the four assets, one or two lose in every square. Remove them and hold the rest. Spotting a loser is easier than picking a winner; it is how a bettor beats the track, by crossing out the horses with zero chance. The job, in his words, is to avoid the huge falls, the bear markets that take 45% to 80% of a portfolio in real terms and 10 years to repair. Since 1945, every one of those began with a jump in the price of energy.

Inflation does one more thing worth understanding early, because it explains how prices can halve while companies keep earning. When prices are stable, buyers pay about 20 years of profit for a share. When inflation appears they stop trusting the far future and pay 15 years, then 10, then 5 at the bottom of the worst markets.

Gave's response is to hold what pays you soon: companies with 4% to 7% dividends and sound management, at a time when a Swiss bond pays 1%. He keeps 25% in energy shares for a double reason. They gain when oil gets expensive, which is the moment everything else suffers, and they pay the dividend while you wait.

The word "risk" misleads

Finance calls price movement risk. A share that moves 2% in a day is risky; a bond that pays the same coupon every year is safe. Détente says this confuses two different things, and Gave says the same in his own words: volatility is movement, and risk is the chance you are never paid back.

Détente calls that second thing danger. The S&P 500 moves 15% to 20% in a normal year. Bitcoin moves far more. Both numbers describe motion and say zero about whether your capital will exist in ten years.

Three pictures make it clear. A bond from a badly run state pays every year, then one Monday a minister announces it pays zero: low movement, total danger. A salary arrives every month for years and stops on a single morning. A taxi driver's income swings with the seasons and the fashion weeks, and Paris keeps hailing taxis: high movement, low danger.

Madoff is the extreme case, and the useful one. Détente's test is that markets refuse the trade he offered: high return with zero movement is a sign someone is lying. Accept the movement, then. It is the price of return. Danger is the thing to refuse, and there are two tools for it. Collateral: a building keeps a value in most worlds. Liquidity: a digital asset trades 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, so you can leave before the door shuts.

This is where prudence turns into a trap. Prudence, as sold, means repeating what worked over the last 40 years. That is a bet that the world stands still, made at the moment the century says it is turning. Détente's image is three generations. The grandfather is an eagle, the son a falcon, the grandson a pigeon, and pigeons get plucked. The grandfather took risk and was called a speculator. The grandson hires prudence and loses the house slowly.

Taking the risk of change gives you 20% odds of surviving it. Refusing the risk gives you 0%.Détente, from the 80% of civilisations that are gone

Gave arrives at the same place through Warren Buffett: big bear markets are the moment capital returns to its rightful owners, the people who did the work and have the time. The point of all this study is to be one of them.

What to own when the ground moves

If the era is late, the season is inflationary and movement is the price of return, the question becomes practical. Détente carries a compass with four points, and Gave's rules fit inside it.

Value moves

The car ruined the cart maker. Value goes to new things, and today it goes faster: technologies are adopted along S-curves, and the curves now overlap instead of waiting their turn. Détente gives two examples. AI unlocks robotics and biology: AlphaFold folded 200 million proteins in 15 days. Computing power is turning into an asset class, and Larry Fink of BlackRock has said so out loud. A new asset class arrives about every 30 years. Gave supplies the physical version: an economy is energy transformed, and the datacenters need more power than the grid has built.

The bet on where value goes is the one bet that is yours alone. It means choosing, and choosing means being wrong sometimes.

Antifragile

Nassim Taleb's word, measured by Didier Darcet. A fragile asset earns when the world is calm: a stock index, a salary. An antifragile asset earns when the world shakes: gold, the yen, Bitcoin, a flat in Geneva or Dubai. Each time Europe shakes, wealthy Europeans move house, and Swiss prices rise. Plot return against volatility and the two kinds of asset slope in opposite directions.

Most assets are fragile, and in calm decades that is fine. In a decade of avalanches you want the other kind. Détente's picture: snow falls quietly for months, then 30 seconds of avalanche reshape the mountain. Revolutions, wars and currency collapses are short, and they do the reshaping.

Decorrelated

Milton Friedman said finance has one free lunch: assets that move for different reasons. Add three waves that peak at different times and the sum is flatter than any of them. Your worst loss shrinks and your return stays. Bitcoin is the least correlated asset over long series. The trap is a portfolio of 500 American shares that feels diversified and falls as one.

Your brain fights this. A flat in Singapore, a datacenter in Texas and a Bitcoin treasury company in one portfolio offend every specialist in the room. Détente takes the offence as the sign it is working.

Sized

A bet is a proportion, and the proportion replaces the yes or no. His example: the market value of gold against the market value of Bitcoin gives about 2%. Put 2% in. Lose it and you live. Win and the portfolio doubles. Three pockets returning 40%, 30% and 20% a year can lose one to zero and repay it with the other two inside a year.

Where to keep it

The world is splitting into blocs, the United States and China first. Détente names the places where the blocs still talk: Switzerland, Singapore, Dubai, Miami, Hong Kong. He calls them hinge countries. Property there rises when the countries around them shake. Gave says it in older words: keep your money where people are left alone, and when gold leaves a country, leave with it, because gold knows first.

Gave adds a structural point that a beginner can hold onto. For 30 years the world ran on zero inventory: buy anything anywhere tomorrow, keep the cash in Treasuries meanwhile. Now countries stockpile rice, copper and oil, and capital that once flowed sits frozen in warehouses. That pushes real interest rates up. Saving pays again, and property bought with cheap loans suffers. He expects to be paid 6% to save, and he plans to save more.

One way to build it

Détente manages his own money in three pockets, which he calls burrows. Computing power: energy plus datacenters in Texas, bought at book value, at a time when CoreWeave trades at 11 times book. Luxury homes off plan in hinge cities, for people who live in two to four countries a year. Bitcoin, amplified through treasury companies, with about 10% of the pocket in call options for convexity. Each pocket passes the four tests. Value is moving toward it, it gains in a storm, it moves for its own reasons, and its size is one he can lose.

Under that sits Gave's base: stocks, gold, cash and bonds picked by the square, a quarter in energy, dividends when inflation runs. Under everything sit Dalio's two rules, which he wrote for a country and which fit a household: earn more than you spend, and treat each other well.

And one rule from Détente for the road. Hold strong convictions, and change your mind the day the conditions change. The blow that ends a business comes from the risk that was left off the list. The map below is the whole argument on one page, and it is meant to be argued with.
