The Exercise

Three maps, learned by playing with them.

Definition
danger
noun. the chance that a thing you own becomes worth nothing.

Not the same as risk. Risk is how much a price moves. Danger is whether the thing survives at all. Almost everybody measures the first one, calls it caution, and then gets killed by the second.

This page is a study of three people who spend their own money and have thought unusually hard about why the next twenty years will not reward the habits of the last forty. Ray Dalio gives you the long arc of history. Charles Gave gives you a map of the four weathers your money can live in. Richard Detente gives you the difference between a bumpy ride and a trapdoor.

Ten lessons, counting from zero, and then one exercise of your own. Six of them you can play with rather than just read. You need to know nothing at all to start. If you would rather read the same material straight through, it is written out in the principles.

The trap of the
last forty years.

Almost every piece of beginner advice you have ever read is a summary of what worked between 1980 and 2020. Buy the index. Hold some bonds. Stay calm. Do not touch it. For forty years that was very close to a free lunch, because interest rates fell almost every single year, which quietly lifted the price of nearly everything, and because money was broadly trusted.

Richard Detente has a word for taking that recipe forward without checking whether the kitchen changed. He calls it prudence, and he means it as an insult. His definition: prudence is applying the recipes that worked for the last forty years to the next forty, so that if it fails, nobody can accuse you of doing anything strange.

The recipe was not wrong. A recipe is an answer to a question, and the question was: what should I own while rates fall for forty years and money is trusted? If that stops being the question, the answer is not wisdom anymore. It is a habit.

Ray Dalio puts the same point in a sentence worth memorising. To understand what is coming at you, you need to understand what happened before you. Not the last forty years. The last five hundred.

So this course does three things, in order. It teaches you to tell a dangerous thing from a merely bumpy one. It gives you a map of which weather you are standing in. And it gives you the long arc that decides which weather you get.

LESSON 01 · DETENTE

Risk is not
danger.

In finance, the word risk almost always means volatility: how much a price jumps around. Something that moves two percent in a day is called risky. Something that sits perfectly still is called safe.

Detente's objection is that volatility measures comfort, not survival. It tells you how uncomfortable the ride is. It tells you nothing about whether the vehicle has a floor. So he splits the idea in two, and refuses to let one word do both jobs.

  • ▸RISK. How much the price moves. This matters if you might need the money on one specific Tuesday.
  • ▸DANGER. The chance it goes to zero and never comes back. This matters always.

The trap is that the two often point in opposite directions. A government quietly heading for bankruptcy pays you the exact same coupon every year, right up until the Monday morning a minister announces there will be no more coupons. Volatility near zero. Danger enormous. Argentina has done this nine times.

A salary is the purest version. Same number, same day, every month, for years, and then it is gone in a single conversation. Meanwhile a self employed driver in Paris has income that swings violently between fashion week and August, and is far less likely to ever reach zero, because people do not stop needing rides.

One more thing worth carrying for life. The link between movement and return is not a convention, it is closer to a law. You can absolutely lose money on a violent asset. What you cannot have is a calm asset that pays a lot. That is why Bernie Madoff was catchable long before he was caught. He claimed ten percent a year in a straight line, and the universe does not draw that line. Anyone offering a lot of return with no movement is either lying or standing on a trapdoor.

LESSON 02 · GAVE

The map:
four weathers.

Charles Gave has spent forty years reducing the whole problem to two questions. Everything financial reacts mostly to two forces: is the economy growing, and is money losing its value. Two questions, two possible answers each, four boxes. He calls them the four quadrants.

The reason this is powerful for a beginner is that it kills the most common bad question in investing. People ask whether gold is a good investment, or whether bonds are safe. Neither question has an answer. An asset is not good or bad in itself. It is good or bad in a weather. Government bonds are the single best thing to own in one of these boxes and a slow disaster in another. Gold is almost exactly the reverse.

LESSON 03 · GAVE

Two dials
nobody can fake.

A map is useless if you cannot tell where you are standing. So how do you know which box you are in?

Not from official numbers. Gave started in 1978 by trying to read growth from GDP and industrial production, and gave up, because the figures get revised, and because they are produced by people with an interest in the answer. It took him forty years to land on something better. The something better is prices, because prices are voted on every second, in public, by millions of people with their own money at stake.

DIAL ONE · ARE WE GROWING?

Divide the S&P 500 by the price of oil. Take a seven year average. The reasoning underneath it is one sentence: the economy is transformed energy. Energy goes in, products come out, and listed companies are the machine doing the transforming. If the machine is turning energy into profit, the ratio climbs. If energy is costing more than the value it creates, the ratio falls. That puts you on the right side of the map or the left.

DIAL TWO · IS MONEY LOSING VALUE?

Divide the price of gold by the price of long dated government bonds. Take a seven year average. You have exactly two ways to park value for later. One is offered by the state, and that is its bonds. The other is the one you use when you no longer believe the state, and that is gold. This ratio tells you which one the world is currently choosing. That puts you in the top half of the map or the bottom.

Why seven years and not five: he tested it against a hundred years of history. Five and six also work, but they throw more false signals. Seven catches the genuine turns and ignores the noise. This is not a trading tool. It moves a handful of times in a working life, and 1980 to 2020 was one single unbroken reading.

The reason he trusts these two above everything else is that there is no committee anywhere that sets the price of gold or the price of oil. Compare that to an inflation statistic, which is assembled by people who would prefer a particular number.

That is the correct posture. The map does not predict. It tells you which weather you are standing in and what tends to break in it.

LESSON 04 · GAVE

Manage by
exclusion.

Most people build a portfolio by inclusion. Collect good things, hope for a good result. Gave says this is backwards, because you are far worse at guessing what will rise than at spotting what is about to be crushed.

His analogy is the racetrack. You do not need to know which horse wins. You need to know which three or four have no chance at all, cross them out, and split your money across what is left. Do that and you beat the crowd more often than the people trying to pick the winner.

In practice it means four families: shares, bonds, gold, cash. In almost any weather, one or two of them are obviously going to be hurt. Remove those. Hold the other three. You have not predicted anything. You have removed the way you get hurt. Gave's own portfolios usually hold three of the four, sometimes only two.

THE SECOND IDEA · DURATION

Duration is how far into the future you are buying. A company priced at twenty times its earnings is a claim on roughly twenty years of them. The same company at ten times is a claim on ten. When inflation arrives, people stop being able to see far ahead, so they shorten the claim: twenty becomes fifteen, then ten, and at the bottom of the truly ferocious markets, five.

Read that again, because it is the least understood thing in investing. Nothing about the company has to change for the price to fall by three quarters. Same profits, same product, same staff. The multiple did all the damage, because the tunnel of visibility got short.

So when your visibility shortens, shorten your duration. That is why dull companies paying five percent dividends become interesting in bad weather. You are being paid now instead of promised later. Gave's example was preferring a Swiss food company yielding five percent over a rocket company priced at a hundred times revenue.

THE THIRD IDEA · NARROW MARKETS ARE A WARNING

He would far rather be in a market where eighty percent of shares rise ten percent than in one where ten percent of shares rise eighty percent. The second one has a name. It is a bubble, and inside a bubble nobody can work out the correct price of anything, because the only remaining reason to buy is that everyone else is buying and you cannot bear to miss it.

And a line he borrows from Warren Buffett, which is the best possible thing to hold on to during a crash: a great bear market is the moment capital returns to its rightful owner. The people who did the work and had the patience to wait.

LESSON 05 · DALIO

The big
cycle.

Ray Dalio has been investing at a global macro level for about fifty years, and his hardest lesson is that the events that hurt him most were the ones that had never happened in his lifetime. So a few years ago he noticed three of them happening at once.

  • ▸One. Debts so large that even cutting interest rates to zero was not enough, so central banks started printing money to cover them.
  • ▸Two. Serious internal conflict from widening gaps in wealth and values, showing up as populism and polarisation on both the left and the right.
  • ▸Three. A rising power getting close enough to challenge the leading one.

He had never seen those three together, so he went looking, and studied the last five hundred years: the Dutch, British, American and Chinese empires, plus Spain, Germany, France, India, Japan, Russia and the Ottomans, and Chinese dynasties back to the year 600.

They had all happened together before. Many times. And almost every time, they marked the changing of a world order. The last time this exact combination lined up was 1930 to 1945.

He measures an empire's power with eight things: education, invention and technology, competitiveness in global markets, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, the power of its financial centre, and the strength of its currency as a reserve currency. They rise in roughly that order, each one pulling the next, and they fall in roughly that order too. Because they are measurable, you can look at a country today and see which stage it is in.

The full arc lasts around two hundred and fifty years, with handovers of ten to twenty years in between. The handovers are usually violent, for a simple reason. A leading power does not step aside politely.

INTERACTIVE 04
Walk the cycle
Six stages, in order. Step through them and ask yourself, honestly, which one sounds most like the news you read this morning.

Dalio is careful about one thing and you should be too. Like human lives, no two cycles are identical, and age alone is a bad predictor. Knowing someone is sixty tells you less than knowing whether they smoke. What you want is the vital signs, not the birthday. The eight measures are the vital signs.

LESSON 06 · ALL THREE

They
always print.

In 1971 Dalio was a young clerk on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange. Back then gold was the money used between countries, and paper dollars were cheques written against it. The United States had written far more cheques than it had gold in the vault. As people queued to cash them, the gold drained away.

On a Sunday evening in August, President Nixon went on television and told the world, in very careful language, that the cheques would no longer be honoured. He did not use the word default. That is what it was.

Dalio came in early on Monday expecting a crash. The market opened, there was pandemonium, and it went up. It kept going up, nearly twenty five percent.

He had never seen a currency devaluation, so he dug into history and found 1933. Roosevelt, same problem, same broken promise, same result. In both cases, cutting the link to gold let the country carry on spending simply by printing more paper. More dollars chasing the same amount of real wealth means each dollar buys less, and the price of nearly everything else goes up.

That gave him the principle he still uses. When central banks print a lot of money to relieve a crisis, the value of paper falls and the price of shares, gold and commodities rises. It happened again in 2008 for the mortgage crisis and in 2020 for the pandemic, and his expectation is that it happens again.

Here is the number to carry with you. Of the roughly 750 currencies that have existed since 1700, fewer than twenty percent still exist. And every single one that still exists has been devalued. Not most of them. All of them.

THE SAME CONCLUSION FROM A DIFFERENT ROAD

Gave arrives at the same place through policy rather than history. After the deflationary depression of the 1930s, central banks decided they would never allow that again, so the standing instruction became to print at the first hint of trouble. But if the bottom left box on the map is forbidden by policy, the pressure has to escape somewhere. His conclusion is that what eventually breaks is not shares. It is bonds and the currency itself.

He separates the two things a bond gives you. The return on capital, which is the rent they pay you for lending. And the return of capital, which is getting your money back at the end. He is not primarily worried about rates going up. He is worried that some governments are simply not able to hand the capital back.

Detente turns the same idea into a number you can feel. If the money supply grows around eight percent a year, then anything in that currency returning less than eight percent a year is quietly being shorn, even while the statement shows a gain. And there is almost nothing sold as prudent that returns more than eight percent. This is danger by erosion rather than by one bad Monday, which is exactly why nobody notices it.

LESSON 07 · DETENTE

Fragile and
antifragile.

Systems do not change gradually. They change by avalanche. Most of the time it is snowflakes landing gently on a mountain, one after another, for months. What reconfigures the entire mountain is thirty seconds.

So at any moment you are living in one of two worlds. The belly of the distribution, which is ordinary years where not much happens and which is most of the time. Or the tails, which are revolutions, wars and monetary breakdowns. The tails are very short, very intense, and they do all of the rearranging.

That gives you a definition you can actually measure. Plot an asset's return above inflation against how much the world is shaking, and look at the slope of the line.

  • ▸FRAGILE. Return falls as volatility rises. It pays you nicely in calm weather and it breaks in a storm.
  • ▸ANTIFRAGILE. Return rises as volatility rises. It does nothing for years, then does everything at once.

This is not a mood. It has a slope, and you can compute it.

WHY THOSE EXAMPLES

Gold is the clean case. The less people trust, the more it rises. The Japanese yen is the strange one, and it is worth understanding because it shows that antifragility does not have to come from innovation. Japanese households save enormously. When the world is calm they send that money abroad, which pushes the yen down. When the world is frightening they bring it home, which pushes the yen up. The mechanism is sociology, not technology.

Then the hinge countries. Switzerland and Dubai are places where blocs that are not speaking to each other can still meet, and where wealthy people go when their own country starts to feel hostile to them. Detente's answer to how long Swiss property keeps rising is blunt: until there are no wealthy people left in Europe. Every political shock somewhere else is a bid somewhere hinged.

And bitcoin, which was designed as an answer to a crisis of confidence in money, so it is antifragile by construction. He is careful to say the theory is clear and the track record is still being written.

THE TRAP RUNS BOTH WAYS

Holding antifragile assets through a long calm stretch hurts, badly, because nobody needs what you own. You have invented music streaming before the internet exists. You have a brilliant cholera vaccine and there is no cholera. Detente is blunt about this: you cannot disrupt a normal universe. Innovation only takes hold when something is broken enough that people are calling for help.

Holding fragile assets into a tail event is worse, because that is where the zeros come from. So the first decision is not which asset. It is which world you think you are in. And that is a decision, not a fact. Nobody can make it for you and nobody can tell you afterwards that you were unreasonable.

LESSON 08 · DETENTE

Where value
moves.

Here is the third idea, and the one Detente insists nobody can do on your behalf.

Value is not a substance you store. It is a location, and it moves. His definition is refreshingly simple: value is what other people want enough to hand over something of their own for. Mow your own lawn and it is a chore. Mow your neighbour's lawn for money and value has been exchanged, because he gave up something real to have it done.

Which means that when the car arrives, the cart does not become slightly less valuable. It goes to zero, slowly and then completely, and the entire value has relocated. If you owned cart factories, being careful with them did not help.

He grounds this in physics rather than economics. His argument is that the universe has been running an innovation machine since it started: simple structures giving way to more complex ones, each new layer packing more energy through less matter, and the gap between layers getting shorter every time. You do not have to buy the whole cosmology to take the practical part, which is this: the interval between disruptions is shrinking. A human life used to contain none. It now contains several.

That is exactly why prudence is a trap now and was not one before. For most of history, change was slower than a life. A Roman living through the fall of Rome did not experience a fall. He experienced three hundred years of things being a bit rubbish. You could invest exactly like your grandfather and be right.

THE PARABLE HE KEEPS COMING BACK TO

A master gives three servants different amounts of money and leaves on a journey. Two of them put it to work and double it. The third, frightened of losing what he was given, buries it in the ground and hands it back untouched, pleased with himself for keeping it safe. He is the one who is punished. Not for losing money, but for refusing to take any risk at all.

Detente's version of the same lesson is arithmetic, and bleaker, and more useful. Around 99.9 percent of all species that have ever lived are extinct. Roughly eighty percent of the civilisations that have existed are gone. So if you accept the fight, your odds are poor. But if you refuse the fight in the name of prudence, your odds are zero. Twenty percent beats nothing.

He also names the three generation curse, because it is the same mistake wearing a family name. The grandfather is an eagle, the son is a hawk, and the grandson is a pigeon. The grandfather built by putting his house up as collateral. The grandson is told to preserve, hands it to someone who promises prudence, and the prudence quietly kills it.

THE TWO RULES HE ENDS ON

The first: hold your convictions strongly and promise you will change your mind. You have to move fast in one direction to get anywhere, and you have to eat your hat without complaining when the facts turn. As he says, it might taste fine.

The second is humility about the shape of failure. Every entrepreneur lists the risks to their project, and the thing that eventually kills them is always the one that was not on the list. Someone building a coding services company in 2010 listed labour costs and client concentration. They did not list a chat window arriving in 2022. Most of the world is not merely unknown. It is unknown that it is unknown.

LESSON 09 · DETENTE

Nothing chained
together.

The last piece is the one that makes resilience free, or close to it. Detente's word is decorrelation, and it means owning things that are not chained to each other.

Most people believe they are diversified because they own an index containing pharma, technology, banks and industrials. On the day the index breaks, all of it breaks at the same moment, because it was never four bets. It was one bet wearing four costumes. If a single event can flatten everything you own, you own one thing.

So his own position, which he offers as his position and not as a recommendation, is three burrows chosen to be as unlinked as he could make them: bitcoin, high end property in the hinge countries, and compute and energy infrastructure.

WHAT A REAL BET LOOKS LIKE

The third burrow is worth walking through, because it shows what "where value moves" looks like when it stops being a slogan.

His reasoning in 2022 went roughly like this. Artificial intelligence is about to explode electricity demand. A power grid is a web, and if you push a gigawatt into one point of a web you tear it, so the constraint will not be total energy, it will be density in one place. Permits for new generation take the better part of a decade, and this industry does not have a decade. Therefore the money will eventually have to move toward distributed capacity, even though distributed computing barely existed at the time.

So he bought land and grid connections in Texas and paid for the wait with bitcoin mining, because mining can be switched on and off in seconds. That flexibility made him genuinely useful to the grid: consume when there is surplus and prices are near zero, shut down and get paid to shut down when there is a squeeze. Then convert the sites to computing when the computing demand arrived. Which it did.

You do not need to copy any of that, and you almost certainly should not. What is worth copying is the shape. He named a change he believed in. He found a way to be present for it that paid for itself while he waited. And he agreed in advance about what would prove him wrong.

One number from that section, because it teaches something small and permanent. A pure data centre company trading at eleven times book value means that eleven dollars of your money buys one dollar of actual machines and ten dollars of expectation. Knowing which of the two you are buying is most of the work.

WHERE ALL THREE AGREE

Five things
they all say.

Three men, three completely different methods. A hedge fund founder reading five hundred years of empires. A portfolio manager who reduced everything to two ratios. An entrepreneur building power stations. They disagree about plenty. Here is what survives all three.

01Volatility is not the thing that kills you. Gave and Detente say it outright. Dalio simply demonstrates it: the most frightening financial headline of 1971 was followed by a twenty five percent rally.
02When a state cannot pay, it prints. Every single time. Dalio has five hundred years of receipts, Gave has the policy reason, Detente has the arithmetic of what it quietly does to your savings.
03The weather matters more than the asset. There is no permanently good investment. There is only an investment that fits the box you are standing in.
041980 to 2020 was a regime, not a law of nature. Every rule you inherited was written inside it.
05The one bet nobody can place for you is where value moves next. Refusing to place it is also a bet, and historically a losing one.

And Dalio's answer to what an ordinary person should actually do, which is deliberately unglamorous and which he puts at the very end of a fifty year career: earn more than you spend, and treat each other well. Everything else, the education, the invention, the competitiveness, is just a way of getting to those two.

THE FOUR CLOCKS, ALL RUNNING AT ONCE

The loop above is the slowest clock. Three faster ones turn inside it, and every one of them is a different person's life work. Read this from the outside in.

ROUGHLY 250 YEARS · THE BIG CYCLE · DALIO
What turns: which country writes the rules, and whose money the world saves in. What you do: work out which stage you are standing in, and never assume the current order is permanent. Of the 750 currencies since 1700, fewer than a fifth still exist.
ROUGHLY 80 YEARS · FOUR GENERATIONS · DETENTE
What turns: the appetite for risk itself. Hard times make strong people, strong people make good times, good times make comfortable people, and comfortable people make hard times. He lays it over the American interest rate curve, which climbs until about 1980 and falls to zero after. What you do: notice that prudence is a fashion, and that it is most popular just before it stops working.
3 TO 10 YEARS · THE FOUR QUADRANTS · GAVE
What turns: growth and inflation, and nothing else. What you do: read the two dials, cross off the one or two assets that always get hurt in that box, and hold the rest. This is the only clock on this page you can actually check on a given morning.
DAYS TO WEEKS · THE AVALANCHE · DETENTE
What turns: everything, at once. What you do: nothing, because by then it is far too late. This is the layer you prepare for during the other three, and it is the only one that ever actually settles the bill.

Long cycles set the rules. Generations set the mood. Quadrants set the weather. Avalanches settle the bill. If you remember one line off this page, that is the one, because it tells you which clock any piece of advice you hear is actually talking about.
